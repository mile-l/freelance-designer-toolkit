How to Charge for Extra Revisions Without Losing the Client

Every freelance designer knows the moment: the project is done, the included revision rounds are used up, and the client sends another email — "just one more small change." You know the change is not small. You also know that saying "that will cost extra" feels like risking the relationship.

Here is the truth most designers learn too late: clients do not leave because you charge for extra work. They leave because the charge feels like a surprise. Charge for extra revisions in the right way, and most clients pay without a second thought — and respect you more for it.

Why "free" extra revisions cost you more than money

One free round is never one round. It resets the client's expectation: if round three was free, round four should be free too. Meanwhile the real cost is compounding — not just the hours, but the other work you turned down, the late nights, and the slow erosion of the rate you quoted in the first place.

Run the numbers before the conversation happens. Our Revision Cost Calculator shows what each round costs at your rate, including the hours clients never see (re-opening files, re-exporting, re-presenting). When you know the number, you can state it calmly instead of resenting it silently.

The golden rule: agree the price before the work starts

The only reliable way to charge for extra revisions is to have agreed the mechanism before the project began. That means your contract or quote already says:

"This project includes 2 revision rounds. Additional rounds are billed at $X/hour, with a minimum of 1 hour, and confirmed by email before work begins."

If that sentence is already in the contract, the conversation is not a negotiation — it is a reminder. The client signed it. You are not springing a fee on them; you are following the agreement they approved.

If you are mid-project and the contract says nothing about extra revisions, you can still fix it — but the conversation is harder, and the fee should be introduced as a policy going forward, not a punishment for this round. More on that below.

The exact words: five scripts that work

Use these instead of improvising. Each one is firm, friendly, and gives the client a choice — because a choice feels fair, and fairness is what keeps the client.

1. The standard reminder (contract already covers it)

"Happy to do that! Just a heads-up — we've used the two revision rounds included in the project, so this round will be billed at $75/hour, roughly 1.5 hours based on the changes listed. Shall I go ahead?"

Why it works: it is cheerful, specific, and asks permission. The client can say yes, trim the list, or stop — all three are fine.

2. The fixed-fee option (clients prefer certainty)

"I can do another round for a flat $120, which covers up to 2 hours of changes. Anything beyond that, I'll quote before starting."

Why it works: many clients hate hourly billing because it feels open-ended. A flat fee for one round removes the anxiety.

3. The "new request" reframe

"That's a great idea — it's a bit beyond the original scope, so I'd treat it as a small add-on rather than a revision. I can quote it at $180 and have it back to you Thursday. Does that work?"

Why it works: it validates the client's idea instead of rejecting it, and reframes the charge as a new deliverable, not a penalty.

4. The grace round (use sparingly)

"I'll cover this one as a courtesy since it's a small fix — just so you know, the included rounds are now used up, so any further changes will be billed at my hourly rate."

Why it works: generosity builds loyalty, but only when it is explicitly framed as a one-time courtesy with a clear boundary. An unexplained free round teaches the client that rounds are free.

5. The retainer pivot (for chronic revisers)

"We've done four rounds on this project, and I want to make sure you get the result you're happy with. Would a small block of 5 hours at a reduced rate work for you, so we can keep iterating without a separate quote each time?"

Why it works: it turns a friction point into an upsell. Some of your best retainer clients start as clients who could not stop revising.

How much to charge

There is no universal number, but there are sensible defaults:

  • Your normal hourly rate, minimum 1 hour. This is the simplest and most defensible. If your rate is $75/hour, an extra round costs at least $75.
  • A flat fee per round. Estimate the hours a typical round takes for that project type and multiply by your rate. A logo round might be a $100 flat fee; a website round might be $300.
  • A percentage of the project fee. Some designers charge 10–15% of the project total per extra round. This scales automatically with project size and is easy to explain.

Whichever you choose, state it in the contract and quote the amount before you start the work. Never do the work and send the bill afterwards — that is how relationships end.

What if the client pushes back?

It happens. Common objections and calm answers:

  • "But it's just a small change." "Totally understand — the change itself is quick, but reopening the project, re-exporting everything, and re-checking it across the files takes about an hour. That's what the fee covers."
  • "Other designers do unlimited revisions." "Some do, and their prices usually reflect that. My quote was based on two rounds — if you'd like unlimited revisions, I can re-quote the project that way."
  • "I didn't know there was a limit." "That's on me for not flagging it earlier — the rounds are in the contract on page one. I'll cover this round, and from here on the rate above applies. Fair?"
  • Silence, or a threat to leave. Let it sit. A client who leaves because you charge fairly for your time was never going to be profitable. The clients you want will pay.

Preventing the problem next time

Charging well is mostly a setup problem. Three habits make extra-revision conversations rare:

  1. 1. Show the cost of a round in your quote. "Includes 2 revision rounds (value $300)" makes the included work visible — and the extra work obviously billable.
  2. 2. Use a revision tracker. When a client can see "Round 2 of 2 — used" in black and white, the conversation starts itself. The Freelance Designer Client OS Notion template tracks revision rounds per project automatically — no more arguing from memory. (Gumroad link — add at launch.)
  3. 3. Get feedback in one consolidated list, from one person. Most "extra rounds" are actually the same round arriving in five separate emails from three different people. One contact, one list, one deadline — put it in the contract.

The mindset shift

Charging for extra revisions is not being difficult. It is the thing that lets you keep doing good work for the clients who respect it. Designers who give away unlimited revisions do not end up with happier clients — they end up burned out, resentful, and underpaid, and the work gets worse for everyone.
