Freelance Designer Pricing Guide 2026: Rates by Experience

"How much should I charge?" is the question every freelance designer asks, usually more than once. The honest answer is that there is no single rate — but there are clear patterns. Rates cluster by experience, by type of work, and by how the designer prices. This guide lays out the ranges designers actually charge in 2026, what moves you up within a range, and how to use the numbers without underpricing yourself.

A note before the numbers: these are typical ranges reported by freelance designers in the US and other English-speaking markets in 2025–2026. Rates vary significantly by location, specialization, and client type. A designer in a high-cost city charging corporate clients will sit at the top of a range; a designer working with small local businesses will sit lower. Use the ranges as a map, not a verdict.

Hourly rates by experience level

Junior designers (0–2 years): $35–$65/hour. At this stage you are selling reliability and fresh skills more than deep expertise. Clients hiring juniors expect guidance and hand-holding, and the rate reflects that. Many juniors start at the low end and raise their rate with every second or third client, which is exactly right — your rate should move as your portfolio does.

Mid-level designers (3–6 years): $65–$110/hour. This is where most working freelancers live. You can run a project end to end, manage a client relationship, and deliver without supervision. The wide range reflects specialization: a mid-level generalist might charge $70, while a mid-level designer with a strong niche — SaaS dashboards, packaging, motion — can charge $100 or more.

Senior designers (7+ years): $110–$175/hour. Senior rates are not really about speed, though seniors are faster. They are about judgment: knowing which of five directions is right, spotting the problem the client has not named, and delivering work that survives contact with a real business. Clients paying senior rates are buying the decisions, not the hours.

Specialists and brand strategists: $150–$250+/hour. Designers who combine design with strategy — brand positioning, conversion-focused UX, design systems for product teams — can command rates well above the senior band. At this level, the work is rarely sold by the hour at all; it is sold as a project fee, with the hourly rate as a quiet internal calculation.

If your current rate sits below the band for your experience, that is useful information — but it is not automatically a problem. A lower rate with a full calendar beats a higher rate with no clients. The question is whether you are undercharging while busy. If you are turning work away, your rate is too low. That is the most reliable pricing signal there is.

Project rates: what common jobs actually cost

Hourly rates are only half the story. Most design work is sold as a project, and project prices vary even more widely than hourly rates — because a project price reflects scope, value, and client size, not just time.

  • Logo design: $300–$1,500 for a small-business logo package; $2,500–$10,000+ for a full identity with guidelines, from an experienced designer. The same deliverable, priced by what it is worth to the client.
  • Brand identity package: $2,000–$8,000 for a small business; $10,000–$50,000+ for a company rebrand with strategy, naming, and a full system.
  • Landing page design: $800–$3,000 for a single page; $3,000–$10,000 for a multi-page marketing site.
  • Website design (5–10 pages): $3,000–$12,000, depending on whether you are designing only or also building.
  • UI/UX design: $5,000–$25,000+ per project, usually scoped by screens or by sprint, and often the highest per-project band for freelancers.
  • Social media template set: $300–$1,200 for a batch of templates; a common entry-level project that leads to retainers.
  • Packaging design: $1,500–$8,000 per product line, with higher prices when the design includes dielines, print coordination, or regulatory work.

Notice the pattern: the gap between the low and high end of each band is large, and it is mostly explained by the client, not the designer. A logo for a local café and a logo for a funded startup are different products with different prices, even if the files look similar. Pricing by the client is not unfair — it is how professional services work.

What actually moves your rate up

Specialization. A designer who does everything charges a generalist rate. A designer known for one thing — fintech dashboards, restaurant branding, book covers — charges a specialist rate, because clients with that problem will pay more for someone who has solved it before.

A portfolio that shows results, not just pictures. "I redesigned their checkout and conversion rose 22%" is worth more than ten beautiful mockups. Clients pay for outcomes, and a portfolio that talks about outcomes justifies a higher rate.

Speed, honestly measured. If you can deliver in half the time, a fixed project price makes that speed profitable. But speed only pays if you price by the project. On hourly billing, speed costs you money — one more reason experienced designers move toward fixed and value-based pricing.

Saying no. The fastest way to raise your rate is to have enough demand that you can decline low-paying work. That takes time to build, but every designer who raised their rates successfully will tell you the same thing: the raise happened when they stopped accepting work below the new number.

Geography, used honestly. You do not need to live in San Francisco to charge San Francisco rates — but you do need clients who can pay them. Many designers keep their local cost base and sell to higher-paying markets online, which is one of the genuine advantages of freelance work.

How to raise your rates without losing clients

The fear that stops most designers is losing existing clients. In practice, a well-handled raise loses very few, and the ones it loses are usually the ones you should have let go anyway.

Raise with new clients first. Your next new client gets the new rate. Existing clients keep the old rate for now. This lets you test the higher number with no risk to current income.

Give existing clients notice. A short, matter-of-fact email — "from March, my rate moves from $75 to $90 an hour; projects already quoted stay at the old rate" — is all it takes. Notice and confidence are the whole technique. Apologizing, over-explaining, or offering to negotiate against yourself are what actually cost you clients.

Raise in steps, not leaps. A jump from $50 to $100 in one move feels reckless to you and to the client. A jump from $50 to $65, then to $80 three projects later, feels like a career progressing. Small, regular raises beat rare, dramatic ones.

Tie the raise to something real. "I now include a brand strategy session in every identity project" or "turnaround is now five working days" gives the client a reason to accept the new number. A raise framed as a better service is easier to accept than a raise framed as a higher bill.

The math behind your rate

Whatever your rate, check it against your own numbers once a year. A freelance rate has to cover more than your salary: taxes, software, hardware, insurance, unpaid admin time, holidays, and the gaps between projects. A common rule is that a freelancer needs to charge roughly two to three times the hourly equivalent of a salaried wage to take home the same amount.

Run the numbers the other way too. If you are quoting fixed prices, track your effective hourly rate on every project — the fee divided by the hours you actually worked. If a "good" project earned you $40 an hour, your pricing is telling you something your confidence is not. The bottom line

There is no correct rate, only a rate that matches your experience, your market, and your demand. The designers who earn the most are rarely the most talented in the room — they are the ones who specialize, price by the project, track their effective rate, and raise their prices regularly and without apology.

Start where you honestly are in the ranges above. Then move up one step with your next new client. That single decision, repeated over a year, is worth more than any pricing formula.
