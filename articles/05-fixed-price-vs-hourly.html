Fixed-Price vs Hourly: How to Quote a Design Project

Every freelance designer eventually faces the same question, usually while staring at a blank quote email: should I charge a flat fee for this project, or bill by the hour?

There is no single right answer. Both models work — and both can quietly lose you money if you pick the wrong one for the job in front of you.

How fixed-price quoting works

With a fixed price, you agree on one number for a defined deliverable: a logo package for $1,500, a landing page for $3,000, a brand identity for $5,000. The client knows exactly what they will pay. You know exactly what you will earn — if the scope holds.

Fixed pricing rewards efficiency. If you can do in ten hours what used to take twenty, you keep the difference. Your effective hourly rate climbs as you get faster, which is exactly how a freelance career is supposed to work. It also makes clients comfortable: nobody likes an open-ended invoice, and a fixed number is much easier for a client to get approved by their own boss.

The risk is scope. A fixed price is only fixed if the scope is fixed. "Just one more round of revisions," "can we also try a version in blue," "could you do the social banners too" — each is small on its own, and together they can turn a profitable project into a week of unpaid work. A fixed quote without a clear scope document and a revision limit is not a fixed price at all. It is an hourly rate with no meter running.

Fixed pricing works best when: the deliverable is clearly defined; you have done similar work before and can estimate the effort reliably; the client wants cost certainty to get budget approval; or you are fast at this type of work and want to be paid for the result, not the hours.

How hourly billing works

Hourly billing is simple: you track your time, multiply by your rate, and invoice the total. A $90/hour rate and 20 hours of work is $1,800, and everyone can see the arithmetic.

Its great strength is fairness when the scope is genuinely uncertain. Exploratory work — a brand discovery phase, an open-ended redesign, a client who does not yet know what they want — is almost impossible to price fairly as a flat fee. Hourly billing means you get paid for every hour you actually work, including the ones spent in the third revision meeting that nobody planned for.

The downside is that hourly billing punishes you for getting better. The faster you work, the less you earn. A senior designer who solves a problem in two hours earns less than a junior who takes eight, which is backwards. Clients also dislike hourly billing because it gives them no reason to trust that the project will end — an open-ended clock feels like an open-ended bill, and clients who feel that way start questioning every invoice line. It also only works if you track time honestly and consistently, and if the client trusts your tracking; disputes about hours are the most common source of friction in hourly engagements.

Hourly billing works best when: the scope is genuinely open-ended or discovery-based; the client wants ongoing, flexible access to you; the effort is hard to predict in advance; or the project is small and the overhead of scoping it would cost more than the work itself.

The decision framework

When a new project lands, run it through three questions:

1. Can I describe the deliverable in one sentence? "A logo, two revision rounds, final files in SVG and PNG." If yes, fixed price is on the table. If the honest answer is "we'll figure it out as we go," go hourly.

2. Have I done this before? If you have delivered similar work five times, you can estimate it. If it is a new type of project, your estimate could be off by 50% — and a fixed price turns that error into your problem, not the client's.

3. Who benefits if it goes faster? With fixed pricing, you do. With hourly, the client does. If you are confident you will be efficient, fixed pricing captures that value for you. If the project is likely to drag — many stakeholders, slow approvals — hourly protects you.

A useful rule of thumb: fixed price for production, hourly for exploration. The discovery workshop and the "help us figure out what we need" phase: hourly. The defined deliverable that comes out of it: fixed.

The hybrid models most experienced designers actually use

In practice, very few experienced freelancers use pure fixed or pure hourly for everything.

Fixed base + hourly extras. Quote a fixed price for the defined scope — including a stated number of revision rounds — and charge your hourly rate for anything beyond it. This is the most common professional setup, and it is the one clients accept most readily, because the base price is certain and the extras are clearly their choice.

Capped hourly. Agree an hourly rate with a maximum cap: "billed at $90/hour, not to exceed $3,000 without written approval." The client gets cost certainty, you get paid for actual effort, and the cap forces a conversation before the bill surprises anyone.

Day rate. A flat rate per day of work, often used for on-site or intensive engagements. It is simple to explain, easy to invoice, and it sidesteps the "how many hours is a day, exactly" argument. Many senior designers prefer day rates for workshops, sprints, and in-house stints.

Milestone pricing. Break a fixed project into stages — discovery, concepts, refinement, delivery — each with its own price and payment. The client pays as value is delivered, and if the project stops halfway, both sides have been paid fairly for what happened. This pairs naturally with a deposit (typically 50% upfront).

How to price a fixed project without guessing

The most common mistake in fixed pricing is pulling a number out of the air. A defensible fixed price has a method behind it:

  1. 1. Estimate your hours honestly, based on similar past projects — not your optimistic hope, your realistic average.
  2. 2. Multiply by your hourly rate for your baseline cost.
  3. 3. Add a buffer of 15–25% for the revisions, meetings, and small extras that always appear.
  4. 4. Add a value adjustment. If the work will directly make the client money — a sales page, a rebrand before a launch — the price should reflect some of that value, not just your hours. A $500 logo and a $5,000 logo can be the same amount of work; the difference is what it is worth to the client.

Then sanity-check the result against your minimum acceptable rate (the number below which you would rather not take the work), and what the market will bear for this type of project.

The mistakes that cost designers the most

Quoting fixed without a scope document. A price with no written scope is a blank cheque the client gets to fill in. Always list what is included: deliverables, formats, number of revision rounds, and what counts as a revision versus a new request.

Quoting hourly without a time estimate. "I'll bill hourly" with no estimate of total hours is a quote that says nothing. Always give a range: "I expect 15–20 hours at $90/hour, so roughly $1,350–$1,800."

Letting the client choose the model for you. Clients will often ask for hourly because it feels safer to them, or fixed because it feels safer to them. Your job is to recommend the model that fits the work, and explain why in one sentence.

Forgetting revisions in the price. Revisions are the single biggest source of lost income in fixed-price work. If your quote includes two rounds and the client uses five, three of those rounds are unpaid unless your contract says otherwise. Put the number in writing, and have a price ready for extra rounds.

The bottom line

Fixed pricing pays you for results and rewards speed. Hourly billing pays you for effort and protects you from uncertainty. The designers who earn the most mix the two deliberately: fixed for defined deliverables, hourly for open-ended work, and a clear revision and extras policy holding the whole thing together.

Whichever model you choose, the quote itself should be clear enough that the client can say yes without a phone call: a price, a scope, a revision limit, and a payment schedule, all on one page.
