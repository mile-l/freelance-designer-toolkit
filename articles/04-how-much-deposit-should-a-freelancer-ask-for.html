How Much Deposit Should a Freelancer Ask For?

The client loved your proposal, agreed your price, and said "let's go." Then you did the work, sent the invoice — and waited. And waited. Two weeks later the invoice is still unpaid, the client has gone quiet, and you have done 30 hours of work for someone who may never pay.

A deposit would have prevented all of it. Not because the deposit is about the money — although the money matters — but because a deposit is the moment a project becomes real. Until a client has paid something, your project is a nice idea. After they pay, it is a commitment.

So how much should you ask for? And how do you ask without sounding like you do not trust the client?

The short answer: 50% upfront, the rest on delivery

The standard that most experienced freelancers settle on is 50% upfront, 50% on completion. It is simple, it is easy to explain, and it splits the risk evenly: the client has not paid for work they have not received, and you have not worked for free.

But 50% is a starting point, not a rule. The right deposit depends on the size of the project, the client, and how much risk you are carrying:

  • Under $500: ask for 100% upfront. Small projects are not worth chasing invoices over, and a client who will not pay $300 in advance will not pay $300 later either.
  • $500–$2,000: 50% upfront is the sweet spot.
  • $2,000–$10,000: 50% upfront, or split it into milestones — 30% to start, 40% at a defined midpoint (e.g. first concepts approved), 30% on delivery. Milestones are better than a single final payment because they keep cash flowing while you work.
  • Over $10,000: milestone payments, with 30–40% upfront and the rest tied to clearly defined stages. Never let the unpaid balance grow larger than the work you have left to do.

The principle behind every number above is the same: your unpaid exposure should never exceed the value of the work you have not yet delivered. If you have done 80% of the project and been paid 30%, you are financing the client's business. Flip that around.

Why clients resist deposits — and why it is rarely about the money

New freelancers worry that asking for a deposit will scare clients away. In practice, the opposite is true. A client who expects you to work for free is telling you something about how they will treat you for the rest of the project. A deposit request filters them out before you have invested anything.

When a client does push back, it is usually one of three things:

"Our accounts payable only pays net-30." That is a policy about invoices, not about deposits. Reply: "I understand — the deposit is what books the project in my schedule, and the final invoice can go through your normal 30-day process." Many companies can pay a deposit by card on the spot, even when invoices go through procurement.

"We don't pay upfront." Then offer a compromise: a smaller deposit, or a first milestone that triggers quickly — "25% to start, and the next 25% when you approve the first concepts, which will be within a week." If they refuse any payment before delivery, that is a red flag, not a negotiation. Walk away, or accept the risk with your eyes open.

"Can we start and I'll pay next week?" This is the most dangerous version, because it sounds reasonable and friendly. The honest answer is: no. Once you start, your leverage disappears. Say it warmly: "I'd love to get started — as soon as the deposit lands I can block the time in my calendar. It usually takes a day or two on your end, right?"

Notice that none of these replies argue about trust. They frame the deposit as a normal part of how you work — because it is.

What the deposit protects (it is more than non-payment)

No-shows. Clients who have paid a deposit show up to meetings, reply to emails, and approve work on time. Clients who have paid nothing treat your project as optional — because to them, it is.

Scope creep. A client who has already paid half the fee is far less likely to ask for free extras than one who has paid nothing. Money committed is a psychological anchor: the project has a shape, and changing the shape has a cost.

Your calendar. The deposit is what justifies turning down other work for this slot. Without it, you are holding a date for someone who has made no commitment to you.

Cancellation. If a client cancels mid-project, the deposit covers the work you have already done. Your contract should state that the deposit is non-refundable once work has started — that is standard, and it is fair.

How to structure the payments

A clean payment schedule has three properties: it is written in the quote before work starts, each payment is tied to something the client can see, and the final payment comes before final delivery of the files.

A typical structure for a $3,000 brand project:

  1. 1. 50% ($1,500) — on signing. Books the project, starts the work.
  2. 2. 25% ($750) — on concept approval. Paid when the client picks a direction.
  3. 3. 25% ($750) — on delivery. Paid before final files are released.

That last line matters more than people think: never hand over final, usable files before the final payment clears. Show low-resolution previews, watermarked proofs, or a live presentation — and release the source files when the money arrives. This is not distrust; it is the industry standard, and any experienced client expects it.

The exact words: asking for a deposit without awkwardness

Put it in the quote, not in a separate conversation. When the deposit is part of the quote document, it reads as policy, not as a personal request:

Payment schedule

  • 50% ($1,500) due on acceptance of this quote, to reserve the project in my schedule.
  • 50% ($1,500) due on delivery of final files.
  • The deposit is non-refundable once work has begun. Final files are released on receipt of final payment.

If a client asks about it in person or on a call, keep it light and factual:

"I take a 50% deposit to book the project — it's how I reserve the time in my calendar. The rest is due when I hand over the final files. It's the same structure I use with all my clients."

"With all my clients" is doing quiet work in that sentence: it tells them this is not about them, it is simply how you run your business.

What about existing clients and repeat work?

With a client who has paid on time three times in a row, you can afford to be more flexible — a smaller deposit, or net-15 on the final invoice. Trust is earned, and you can price it in. But do not drop the deposit entirely. Even good clients have cash-flow problems, staff changes, and budget freezes that have nothing to do with you. The deposit is not about whether you trust them. It is about whether your rent depends on their accounts department.

One practical rule: if a returning client asks to skip the deposit "just this once," treat it as a new-client conversation. Circumstances change; a client who was reliable last year may be under pressure this year. A short, friendly "I still take a deposit on every project, it keeps my schedule honest" costs you nothing and protects you completely.

The bottom line

The deposit is not a test of the client, and it is not a sign of distrust. It is the moment a project stops being a conversation and starts being a commitment — for both sides. The client commits money, you commit calendar time, and from that moment on you are working together instead of one of you working for free.

50% upfront, 50% on delivery. Write it in the quote. Tie the final payment to the release of final files. And if a client will not pay anything before you start, believe them: they are telling you exactly how the end of the project will go.
