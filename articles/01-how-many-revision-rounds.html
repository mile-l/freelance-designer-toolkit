How Many Revision Rounds Should You Include?

← Back to the free toolkit All guides

If you have ever watched a "quick logo tweak" turn into a fourth round of revisions at 11 p.m., you already know the real question is not how many revisions to include. It is how many you can afford to give away for free.

Most freelance designers answer this question by copying whatever number they saw in someone else's contract: two rounds, three rounds, "unlimited" if they are feeling generous (or desperate). That guess is expensive. Every extra round is unpaid labor, and unpaid labor is the fastest way to turn a profitable fixed-price project into a loss.

This guide gives you a number you can defend — and a way to calculate what each round actually costs you.

The short answer: 2–3 rounds, clearly defined

For most freelance design projects, two rounds of revisions is the industry standard, three is generous, and one is only right for very small, tightly scoped jobs like a single social graphic.

Here is how the numbers break down in practice:

  • 1 round: Best for small deliverables with a fixed brief — a single illustration, a banner, a business card. The client reviews once, you fix what is genuinely wrong, you deliver.
  • 2 rounds: The sweet spot for most projects — logos, brand identities, landing pages, pitch decks. Round one is for direction ("is this the right concept?"), round two is for polish ("move this, resize that").
  • 3 rounds: Reasonable for large, multi-stakeholder projects — a full website, a brand system with many touchpoints. More reviewers means more conflicting feedback, and a third round absorbs it.
  • Unlimited revisions: Almost never a good idea. It signals that the client can iterate forever, and some will. If a client insists on unlimited, the price should reflect it — or walk away.

The number matters less than the definition. "Two rounds" means nothing if the client thinks a round is a single comment and you think it is a consolidated list of fifty changes from five people. Define a round in your contract: one round = one consolidated set of feedback, delivered by one contact person, within a stated number of days.

Why the number matters: what a revision round actually costs

Designers underprice revisions because they price the work, not the rework. A round is not just the hour you spend moving a logo. It is:

  • Re-opening the file and re-orienting — 15–30 minutes you never bill for.
  • The changes themselves — anywhere from 30 minutes to a full day.
  • Re-exporting, re-uploading, re-presenting — another 15–30 minutes.
  • Context switching — the half-day you lose because the project interrupted other work.

Put a number on it. Take your hourly rate and multiply it by a realistic per-round estimate for that project type. A logo revision round might cost you 1.5 hours; a website revision round might cost 4. If your rate is $75/hour, one "free" website revision round is $300 of your time.

How to decide the number for a specific project

Use these four factors instead of a default:

1. Project size and complexity. A single-page site does not need the same revision allowance as a 20-page site. Scale the rounds to the deliverable count.

2. Number of decision-makers. Every additional approver multiplies the feedback. If the client says "the team will review," ask how many people are on the team and who has final say. Three reviewers with no single decision-maker is a red flag — add a round, or raise the price.

3. How clear the brief is. A client with references, a brand guide, and a written brief will need fewer rounds than a client who says "make it pop." Vague briefs deserve more rounds in the contract — and a higher price, because you are absorbing their uncertainty.

4. Your relationship with the client. A repeat client who gives clean, consolidated feedback is cheap to serve. A new client with a history of scope creep is not. Price the risk, not just the work.

What to write in your contract

A number without rules is a loophole. Your revision clause should cover:

  • The number of rounds included — and that it applies per deliverable, not per project.
  • What counts as a round — one consolidated feedback list from one contact, delivered within X business days.
  • What counts as a revision vs. a new request — moving a button is a revision; adding a new page is a new request, billed separately.
  • What happens after the included rounds run out — your hourly rate for additional rounds, stated upfront. "Additional revision rounds are billed at $X/hour" is the single most valuable sentence in a freelance contract.
  • Feedback expiry — feedback received more than X days after delivery is treated as a new request.

The most common mistake: revisions without a deadline

Revisions that arrive six weeks after delivery are a different kind of cost. You have moved on, the files are archived, and re-opening the project costs more than the original round did. Put a window on it: feedback within 5 business days of delivery, or the round is considered used. Clients respect boundaries they can see.

A simple rule to remember

Price your project as if the client will use every revision round you include — because the difficult clients always do. If you include three rounds at $300 of your time each, that is $900 of risk baked into a fixed price. Either the price covers it, or the number comes down.
