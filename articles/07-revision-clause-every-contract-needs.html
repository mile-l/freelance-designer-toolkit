The Revision Clause Every Freelance Contract Needs

Most freelance designers do not lose money because they price too low. They lose money because of one missing paragraph: the revision clause. A project quoted for two rounds of revisions quietly becomes five, and the extra three rounds — the emails, the meetings, the late-night tweaks — are worked for free. A good revision clause prevents that. It is short, it is not hostile, and it is the single most valuable sentence in your contract.

This article explains what a revision clause should say, why each part matters, and how to handle the conversation when a client asks for one more round than the contract allows.

What a revision clause actually does

A revision clause is not legal decoration. It does three practical jobs at once.

First, it defines what counts as a revision. Without a definition, everything is a revision — or nothing is, depending on who is arguing. A clear clause says a revision round is one set of consolidated feedback on the delivered work, returned within a stated number of days, covering changes within the agreed scope. New pages, new features, or a change of direction are not revisions; they are new scope.

Second, it caps the number of rounds included in the price. Two rounds is the industry standard for most design work, and it is a good default. One round is too few for most clients; three or more rounds included in the base price invites endless iteration. The number itself matters less than the fact that there is a number, agreed in advance.

Third, it prices the extra rounds. This is the part designers most often skip, and the part that costs them the most. The clause should state the rate for additional rounds — a flat fee per round, or an hourly rate — so that an extra round is a simple commercial decision, not an awkward negotiation. When the price is already in the contract, asking for a fourth round feels like ordering from a menu instead of asking for a favor.

The five elements of a strong clause

A revision clause that holds up in real projects has five parts. You do not need legal language for any of them; plain English is better, because the client has to understand it and agree to it.

1. The number of included rounds. State it plainly: "This project includes two (2) rounds of revisions." Put the number in digits as well as words — it removes all ambiguity, and it looks deliberate.

2. The definition of a round. "One revision round means one set of consolidated feedback on the work as delivered, provided within five (5) business days of delivery." Consolidated matters: ten separate emails from four different stakeholders are not one round. A deadline matters too, because feedback that arrives six weeks later is not a revision of the project you delivered — it is a new brief.

3. The scope boundary. "Revisions cover changes to the work within the agreed scope. New pages, new features, changes to the brief, or a change of creative direction are additional work and will be quoted separately." This one sentence is what stops "just one more page" from becoming a second project.

4. The price of extra rounds. "Additional revision rounds are billed at $150 per round (or at the designer's hourly rate of $75/hour), payable before the round begins." Charging in advance is important: it turns the extra round into a paid, agreed piece of work rather than a debt you chase later.

5. The approval mechanism. "A round is complete when the client approves the work in writing, or when five (5) business days pass after delivery without feedback." Without an approval mechanism, a project can sit in revision limbo forever, and you cannot invoice the final milestone. This clause lets the project close.

A clause you can copy

Here is a complete clause that combines all five elements. Adapt the numbers to your own rates and timelines, but keep the structure:

Revisions. This project includes two (2) rounds of revisions. One revision round means one set of consolidated feedback on the work as delivered, provided in writing within five (5) business days of delivery. Revisions cover changes within the agreed scope only; new pages, new features, changes to the brief, or a change of creative direction are additional work and will be quoted separately. Additional revision rounds are billed at $150 per round, payable before the round begins. A round is complete when the client approves the work in writing, or when five (5) business days pass after delivery without feedback.

That is roughly 120 words, and it covers the entire dispute that eats most freelancers' margins. It is not aggressive — it reads like a professional process, because it is one.

Why designers resist writing one

If a clause is this useful, why do so many freelancers skip it? The reasons are almost always emotional, not practical.

"It will scare the client off." In practice, the opposite happens. Clients who have worked with freelancers before expect a revision clause; its absence looks unprofessional, not friendly. A client who is scared off by a clear revision policy is a client who planned to ask for unlimited revisions — which is exactly the client the clause protects you from.

"My clients are nice, they won't take advantage." Most clients are nice. They also have bosses, committees, and shifting opinions. Unlimited revisions are rarely a deliberate abuse; they are what happens when nobody set a limit and everyone keeps having one more thought. The clause protects nice clients too, by giving them a clear process instead of an awkward conversation.

"I'll handle it if it comes up." Handling it when it comes up means negotiating under pressure, mid-project, with a deadline looming — the worst possible moment to set a boundary. Setting the boundary in the contract, when everyone is calm and happy, costs nothing.

What to say when a client asks for "just one more round"

Even with a clause in place, the moment will come. A client will ask for one more round, and how you answer matters.

The wrong answer is a resentful yes or a flat no. The right answer points at the contract, warmly and briefly: "Of course — that's an extra round, so it's $150 as we agreed in the contract, and I can start on it as soon as that's confirmed." No apology, no justification, no lecture about the clause. You are not enforcing a rule; you are following the process you both agreed to.

If the client pushes back, you have options that cost you nothing. You can offer a smaller round — a single specific change rather than a full round. You can trade: an extra round in exchange for a testimonial, a referral, or a faster payment schedule. But do not trade the round for nothing. Every free round teaches the client that the clause is decoration, and the next project will have the same problem.

One more practical point: track your revisions. Note how many rounds each project actually took, and what they cost you. If your two-round projects consistently run to four rounds, either your price is wrong, your intake process is letting vague briefs through, or your clients need a firmer round structure. The bottom line

A revision clause is not about distrust. It is about clarity, and clarity is what makes a client relationship survive the messy middle of a project. Two rounds, defined, priced, and closed by approval — five elements, about 120 words, and the difference between a project that pays for itself and one that quietly doesn't.

Add the clause to your next contract before you send it. It takes five minutes, and it is the cheapest insurance a freelance designer can buy.
